Intelligent behavior arises from the activity of billions of neurons interacting through trillions of synapses that are continuously modified by experience. Our group aims to elucidate this process by developing computational theories of learning, memory, and decision-making that causally relate changes at the synaptic, neural, and behavioral levels. A complementary aim is to develop statistical methods to generate compact descriptions of data across these levels. We pursue these goals by building mathematical models informed by neurobiology, and based on artificial neural networks and other machine learning techniques, and testing them in close collaboration with experimentalists.

Examples of questions we seek to answer using theory-driven approach include: **What wiring patterns and plasticity rules enable the brain to rapidly learn meaningful structure from high-dimensional sensory input and motor output? How is the resulting knowledge stored reliably in memory despite ongoing plasticity? How is it used to make better decisions and reduce effort?** Our data-driven methods are geared toward responding to the growing need for characterizing data collected from naturalistic paradigms. **Are there interpretable hidden states (e.g., motivation, exploration etc.) that jointly dictate neural and behavioral dynamics over long timescales? Can we understand inter-individual differences in terms of the relative prevalence of those states?**

Projects in the lab typically involve analyzing data from experimental collaborators and/or simulating mathematical models such as neural networks and Markov decision processes. We welcome students on campus interested in computational neuroscience and comfortable with math and programming to get in touch to discuss research opportunities. If you’re interested in pursuing a PhD in our group, you should apply to the Cognition and Neuroscience PhD program. Prospective postdocs may reach out by email.
